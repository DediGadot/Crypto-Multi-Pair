ABSTRACT 

We develop a multi-country, multi-sector New Keynesian model to examine the drivers of global inflation during the pandemic and recovery periods. The model incorporates both country-sector-specific and aggregate shocks that propagate through international trade and production networks, leading to global demand-supply imbalances. Production complementarities raise non-labor costs, resulting in widespread sectoral cost-push inflation, even though the original negative labor supply shock is heterogeneous across country-sectors. Flexible exchange rate regimes absorb cross-country monetary policy differences, curbing global demand reallocation and the inflationary spillovers relative to fixed-rate regimes. The interaction of sectoral supply and aggregate demand shocks is the key to match the inflation experience of several countries, as the sectoral shocks spread through the global production network. yildirim@gmail.com 1 Introduction 

The Covid-19 pandemic delivered a severe shock to the global economy, triggering inflation rates not seen in the past four decades. The widespread and synchronized nature of inflation across countries is difficult to reconcile with the diverse domestic monetary and fiscal pol-icy responses, multiple economic disruptions, and varying recovery trajectories. We argue that international linkages played an important role in transmitting domestic shocks across borders, leading to global supply and demand imbalances. These imbalances manifest as asymmetric sectoral shocks and led to cost-push inflation. We develop a multi-country, multi-sector New Keynesian macroeconomic model with full global input-output network to quantify the role of international linkages in driving inflation. Our key result is that the primary driver of inflation was strong demand coupled with constrained supply, where international linkages determine the relative contributions of demand and supply to inflation that varies by country. A sectoral approach to inflation in an open economy setting enables us to quantify these differences. Our framework extends the closed-economy models of Baqaee and Farhi (2022) and Guerrieri, Lorenzoni, Straub, and Werning (2022), by incorporating a global trade and pro-duction network, which serves as a crucial transmission mechanism for both sector-level and aggregate shocks across countries. This global network approach is unique to our paper. It distinguishes our contribution from closed-economy network models and open-economy mod-els that typically feature two sectors (tradable and non-tradable). In our framework, similar to closed economy models, a negative supply shock in one sector can reduce demand for goods in another sector (Guerrieri, Lorenzoni, Straub, and Werning, 2022), raising produc-tion costs in the sectors that use the “shocked” sector as an input (Baqaee and Farhi, 2022). In an international context, these negative “Keynesian” supply shocks can spillover both via complementarities in the global trade and production network and also via reallocation of demand globally from certain sectors to others and the role of trade in intermediate inputs in this process. Hence, our model’s key contribution is to provide a structure for reliable quantitative estimates, by incorporating a rich set of realistic shocks, empirically relevant trade and production elasticities, and several channels for the international transmission of these shocks. The paper’s baseline quantitative exercise delineates three distinct phases in the evolution of global inflation. In the early phase of 2020, supply shocks arising from pandemic-induced scarcity in factors of production sparked inflation. This period was characterized by local and global supply chain bottlenecks, rising factor costs, including prices of imported intermediate inputs, and slack in domestic labor markets. In the next phase, demand and supply imbal-2ances were further amplified by large fiscal packages and loose monetary policy, particularly in advanced economies. These aggregate demand shocks along with economies suddenly re-opening helped to spur on inflation over 2021. The final phase of 2022 was characterized by the Russian invasion of the Ukraine with a sizeable impact on energy prices. Importantly, we quantify considerable heterogeneity in the spillover of shocks across economies, such that the international drivers of inflation were more important for the euro area than for the US. 

Model framework. The model is constructed using intertemporal and intratemporal blocks. The intertemporal block is a standard two-period open-economy macro model. We assume that households in each country have perfect foresight. They have access to a local currency domestic bond and can also trade a nominal bond denominated in US dollars in-ternationally. 1 Nominal exchange rate changes are solved for given standard asset market conditions where the Uncovered Interest Parity (UIP) and households’ Euler equations hold. Current accounts adjust endogenously to shocks such that capital flows across countries equi-librate world financial markets. 2 We close this demand block by allowing for each country to set domestic policy rates exogenously. The intratemporal block has a static nested CES production structure with multiple factors of production and a global production network, which plays a key role in the interna-tional transmission of shocks. The network adjusts endogenously in the model once we feed in the shocks, as all relative prices adjust and sectoral expenditures also change. The only frictions in the model, downward wage rigidity, and segmented factor markets are impor-tant for this block. Segmented labor markets are especially important as we assume a fully inelastic labor supply in each labor market. We motivate these rigidities by the evidence presented in Fernald and Li (2022), that during the period we focus on (2020-2022), the con-tribution of labor reallocation from low to high wage/productivity sectors was very small, 0.18 percent, whereas labor productivity grew 1.1 percent. These data-relevant assumptions of segmented labor markets and short-run rigid wages also help us to match the 2020 rise in unemployment. 3 The existence of segmented factor markets also helps us to match cross-sectional moments such as changes in sectoral wages and product prices. The demand side in the intratemporal block allows households to access goods and services globally through a nested CES demand structure.

3Approximating inflation in an open economy. Before our quantification exercise of the non-linear model, we start by providing a first-order approximation of domestic inflation that extends the closed-economy result in Baqaee and Farhi (2022) to the open economy. This provides a novel contribution and delivers intuition for our two key results. First, the approximation shows that the impact of foreign factor supply shocks on domestic inflation depends on domestic consumption’s exposure to foreign factors of production. This exposure to foreign factors does not only depend on traded intermediates, as shown in other sufficient statistics type approaches, but rather, it depends on the full set of global input-output linkages, implying a dependence on foreign labor through complementarities in production. This foreign-factor exposure varies greatly across countries. 4

Second, the approximation contains an additional term relative to the closed-economy approximation, which we call “local-global demand-supply imbalance”. This term captures the discrepancy between local and global changes in factor demands (via consumption of final and intermediate goods through the global input-output network). For example, suppose there is a rise in demand for factors embedded in “microchips” (an intermediate good) used in the production of “iPhones” (a final good). The imbalance appears when the final demand for “iPhones” across countries outweighs the global supply of “microchips” given a shortage in the underlying factors of production. If, however, all the countries had exactly the same demand profiles (e.g., no home bias), then this term would be zero. This term allows us to understand the inflationary impact of mismatches between demand structures as well as foreign sectoral supply shocks working through sectoral demand-supply imbalances within the global trade and production network. This result is similar in spirit to the welfare gains from trade shown in Baqaee and Farhi (2024), but highlights the impact of changing factor shares, over time and across countries, on inflation for open economies. 

Quantitative approach. Our quantitative analysis provides a decomposition of the drivers of inflation through the lens of our model. We first use the model structure to back out a set of sectoral and aggregate supply and demand shocks for each country from the data. We calculate five shocks at the quarterly level: sectoral consumption demand, monetary policy, aggregate demand, sectoral labor supply, and energy prices. Sectoral demand shocks are modeled as preference-shifters across sectors, which we back out from the data using sectoral consumption shares. Changes in monetary policy are based on observed changes in policy rates of countries. Aggregate demand shocks are intertemporal shifters that we back out using information on monetary policy rates and local currency aggregate expenditures.

Given our downward nominal wage rigidity, the model will classify sectors as labor supply or demand constrained. Supply constrained sectors are sectors where supply meet demand at a level equal or higher to the nominal wage lower bound. Main results. The model is successful in generating aggregate inflation series that track headline numbers. Once we shock each quarter, we calculate deviations in all endogenous variables from their steady-states. We then compute year-on-year inflation rates for ag-gregate prices in each quarter, which provides a time series for the evolution of inflation. Specifically, the correlation between the model-generated series and actual headline inflation is 0.75 and 0.77 for the US and the euro area, respectively. These correlations increase to 0.86 and 0.91 if we drop the lockdown and rebound quarters (2020Q2 and 2021Q2, respec-tively) that were unique in several dimensions and hard to match even with our rich set of shocks. The model can also match the evolution of real wages together with the cross-sectional patterns in sectoral wages and prices. Given the rich production structure, we find this cross-sectional fit of the model comforting. Finally, the model also generates exchange rate changes and current account dynamics that are consistent with the data. A model-based decomposition over 2020–2022 reveals the following. In 2020, negative sectoral factor supply shocks – taken as falling labor supply in our context – played the primary role in both the US and the euro area, the countries that observed the fastest rise

In 2021, positive aggregate demand shocks played a central role and boosted inflation significantly, contributing 8.53 and 5.79 pp to annual inflation over this year for the US and the euro area, respectively. These numbers might suggest domestic aggregate demand shocks are more important than supply shocks in driving inflation in all countries. Our decomposition between domestic and international sources of inflation reveals that this would be a misleading conclusion since spillovers of supply shocks (especially energy prices), are central. We show that while the extent of international spillovers is limited from other countries to the US, both international aggregate demand shocks and energy price spillovers played a predominant role in the euro area’s inflation. To further assess the role of international spillovers, we conducted two additional counter-factual exercises. First, we study how foreign sectoral supply shocks affect inflation with higher trade elasticities. Trade elasticities should play an important role as they dictate how much a fall in the supply of goods from one country-sector can be substituted with varieties from other country-sectors. Unsurprisingly, a higher trade elasticity (its long-run value of 4) amplifies the inflationary impact of foreign supply shocks as demand switches to home country’s factors of production. Second, we examine the impact of exchange rate regimes. When switching from our baseline scenario of flexible exchange rates to a fixed exchange rate system pegged to the U.S. dollar, we observe higher inflation. This outcome is expected, as under a fixed exchange rate, changes in U.S. monetary policy directly influence domestic policy rates in other countries, amplifying inflationary spillovers. Thus, flexible exchange rates helped mitigate pandemic-era inflation by dampening the transmission of US demand shocks to the rest of the world. 

Related literature. Our multi-sector approach to inflation is shared by the closed-economy papers of Guerrieri, Lorenzoni, Straub, and Werning (2021), Baqaee and Farhi (2022), Rubbo (2023), Lorenzoni and Werning (2023). We share with these papers the idea that relative prices changes can generate inflation. We emphasize the open-economy dimension of this channel, where domestic supply shocks traveling through network imply different relative price changes and hence different inflation experiences in each country, globally. As in single-sector closed economy models of Blanchard and Bernanke (2023) and Gagliar-done and Gertler (2023), we also highlight the role of oil shocks, product price increases, and labor market tightness, though in an open-economy context, helping us to share the insight 6with these papers that strong demand meeting constrained supply is an important feature of this period, that is, supply chain bottlenecks alone and/or large stimulus packages alone is not enough, but rather we need the interaction of the two, in order to understand the inflationary experience of the last few years. In terms of the open-economy papers studying the recent inflationary episode, our paper is quite different. The work by Amiti, Heise, Karahan, and S ¸ahin (2024), Comin, Johnson, and Jones (2023), Ferrante, Graves, and Iacoviello (2023), Guerrieri, Marcussen, Reichlin, and Tenreyro (2023), Fornaro and Romei (2023) and Bianchi and Coulibaly (2024), are all in the tradition of Gali-Monacelli type models, where there are one or two sectors with standard nominal rigidities and no I-O linkages. Ferrante, Graves, and Iacoviello (2023) is an exception with full network but focusing only on a small open economy. Our global general equilibrium with the full global I-O linkages combined with both supply and demand shocks, help us to highlight new channels of international spillovers theoretically and quantitatively in addition to standard role of exchange rate and demand allocation in these papers. We are not aware of any other structural quantification exercises for the pandemic-era inflation in a global setting, but there are reduced-form empirical studies that seek to identify the different drivers of the U.S.inflation with sign restrictions in VAR, such as Jord´ a and Nechio (2022), Jord´ a, Liu, Nechio, and Rivera-Reyes (2022), Shapiro (2022a,b); de Soyres, Gaillard, Santacreu, and Moore (2024); Giannone and Primiceri (2024). 6

## 2.3 Monetary Policy, Nominal Variables and the Current Account 

In this subsection, we describe how we determine nominal variables such as the exchange rate and world expenditure, which are necessary to solve for equilibrium across countries. We also discuss the current account determination in the model. 

2.3.1 Nominal Variables Exchange rate determination. The framework described above is, in essence, a canon-ical international macroeconomic model that embeds a disaggregated production and trade structure. The nominal exchange rate is a key relative price to help equilibrate goods and assets markets across countries. The nominal exchange rate of each country n relative to the United States is determined by the international arbitrage equation (5), that is by the interest rate differential. This is a classical no-risky-arbitrage condition (Uncovered Interest Parity). It immediately tells 11 us that if nominal exchange rates are flexible, they are determined by the relative stance of monetary policy via interest rate differential between the home country and the United States, which acts as the num´ eraire currency country. 9

In our quantitative model, nominal interest rates are exogenous since we assume monetary policy is exogenous. For these reasons, we chose to take a conservative approach and not introduce any shock to TFP at the sector-country level, except for Russia’s energy sector starting in 2022Q1, for the reasons we explain next. 

Energy price changes. We introduce energy price changes, illustrated in Figure D.3, as a negative TFP shock for the Russian energy sector starting in 2022Q1, i.e., as a decline in log AEnergy, Russia . While we do have information prior to 2022Q1 for energy prices, their source of variation cannot be attributed to supply and demand. In contrast, starting in 2022Q1 the Russia-Ukraine war provides a source of variation on energy prices that we attribute to a supply force that reduced the capacity of the Russian energy sector to produce, isomorphic to a negative TFP shock. We emphasize that our shock is at the sector-country level: we introduce it as a TFP shock to the Russian energy sector; it is not a symmetric TFP shock to energy sectors worldwide. This is for two reasons. First, all prices are endogenous in our setup; hence, a “price shock” does not exist. Second, this approach allows us to capture spillovers to other country-sector pairs when the energy shock has a clear country-sector origin. This contrasts with the literature that considers an energy price shock as an aggregate world-level shock. Thus, our model provides a lower bound for the effects of energy price shocks on inflation across countries.

## 4.3 International Spillovers of Demand and Supply Shocks 

4.3.1 Decomposition 

We start with the decomposition of the “all shocks” inflation numbers that were generated by the model shown in Figure 3. To do so, we re-estimate the model by applying each shock one-by-one (for all countries). Figure 7 shows the output for these exercises for the US and euro area in panels (a) and (b), respectively. Before describing the full sets of results, we clarify two important points. First, in what follows, we refer to our measures of changes in sectoral hours worked simply as “sectoral supply shocks.” We do so because we do not use any TFP shock measures at the sectoral level, and therefore, except for the Russian energy shock, the only supply shocks we consider are our sectoral-level hours worked changes. Second, note that the sum of predicted inflation rates of the different “shock experiments” need not equal the inflation rate of the “all shocks” model results reported above, since those solutions capture non-linear interactions generated by applying all shocks simultaneously. We begin by considering the impact of sector-level supply shocks (the purple dots) in isolation. Two main patterns emerge both for the US and euro area. First, sector-level supply shocks were inflationary early in the pandemic. Thus, in the absence of these shocks, there would have been more disinflation early on than observed in the data. Second, we see that without the expansion of sector-level supply in early 2022 as supply chain bottlenecks began to clear up and workers began returning more to the labor market, inflation would have been even higher in the US. We now examine the role of sector-level demand shocks (the yellow crosses). These shocks capture the consumption switching across sectors that took place as economies closed and then reopened, between manufacturing and services. Not surprisingly, the substitution to goods consumption early in the pandemic had an inflationary effect, albeit small, but surprisingly, the re-balancing later as the economy reopened did not have a disinflationary effect. This result highlights the importance of asymmetric shocks linked to global demand-supply imbalances. The original inflationary impact of this consumption reallocation shock is also much smaller compared to the impact of the negative sectoral supply shocks, a result that is not obvious a-priori. Aggregate demand shocks (pink plus signs) played an important role in driving inflation over the period. Notably, the model captures the impact of the fall in aggregate demand and its disinflationary forces early on in the US, as is also found in Baqaee and Farhi (2022). 35 Figure 7. 4.3.3 The Role of the Trade Elasticity 

We next investigate the quantitative importance of changing trade elasticities of substitution within the global network on the amplification/dampening of shocks on domestic inflation. We focus on how varying these elasticities changes supply shock transmission across coun-tries. To do so, we set the trade elasticities of substitution to 4 while keeping the production elasticities of substitution at their baseline values. We then introduce foreign sectoral supply shocks while setting domestic sectoral supply shocks to zero to isolate the impact of the change in trade elasticities on the transmission of foreign supply shocks. The intuition for this result is as follows. Foreign sectoral supply shocks increase relative prices in the rest of the world relative to the home economy. In response, countries switch their expenditure towards cheaper goods from the home economy thus putting inflationary pressure at home. This effect is larger with higher trade elasticities of substitution and would explain the higher inflation in 2020 for the ‘Trade substitution’ scenario. Since supply shocks reversed over the period in 2021–2022, the opposite expenditure switching happened: with improvements in labor markets and a decline in relative prices in the rest of the world, countries (both home and foreign) shifted their demand towards foreign goods. This resulted in deflationary pressures for the domestic economy in 2022. Importantly, a higher trade elasticity is counterfactual in the short-run. If we use these higher trade elasticity for the baseline, then, our figure 3-model prediction would have shifted up predicting much higher inflation than the observed series, especially early on.

To understand the intuition, let us focus on the early period and ignore all other shocks but interest rate changes. By shutting down discount factor shocks ( ˆϕn = 1), countries’ aggregate demand, which in our model takes the form of local currency expenditure ( ˆEn), depends on monetary policies only. Under flexible exchange rates, euro area aggregate demand depends on domestic interest rate changes only (see equations (4) and (5)). The nominal exchange rate absorbs interest rate differentials, insulating euro area aggregate demand from US monetary policy. In contrast, under a fixed exchange rate, euro area aggregate demand depends on US monetary policy only as the euro area gives up its monetary policy stance. It is now entirely exposed to US monetary policy changes. Since the euro area’s monetary policy was less expansionary than that of the US early in the pandemic, the euro area aggregate demand declined more under flexible exchange rates relative to fixed exchange rates, leading to higher deflation (in absolute value). The reverse logic applies in 2022, explaining the faster decline in inflation. Panel (b) of Figure 12, where we introduce only interest rate changes, confirms this intuition. Panel (c) and (d) of Figure 12 provide further evidence on the mechanism under aggregate demand shocks (discount factor changes) and sectoral supply shocks only. We observe the same patterns where fixed exchange rates deliver higher inflation early on and faster inflation decline in 2022.

42 5 Conclusion 

The prevailing narrative on pandemic inflation is as follows: In 2020, an asymmetric shock induced by the pandemic prompted a shift in consumption from services to goods. The subsequent recovery period (2021–2022) was characterized by a mix of fiscal stimulus and varying monetary policies, accompanied by an energy shock in 2022 linked to Russia’s in-vasion of Ukraine. By 2023, most countries managed to control inflation without entering deep recessions, achieving what has been termed a ”soft landing.” We contend that a multi-sector, multi-country global network New Keynesian model can both capture this narrative and quantify the critical role of international linkages in transmitting domestic shocks and policies. Our model incorporates both sector-specific and aggregate demand and supply shocks that propagate through international trade and pro-duction networks, leading to global demand–supply imbalances. It successfully replicates aggregate trends in inflation, real wages, exchange rates, and current accounts, as well as the cross-sector dynamics of wages and prices. A key insight from our analysis is that the interaction between sectoral supply shocks and aggregate demand shocks is central to explaining the heterogeneous inflation experiences across countries, as these shocks diffuse through global trade and production networks. The differential impacts of demand and supply shocks on various economies can be rational-ized through the new channels of international spillovers documented in our study. Due to the complementarities inherent in global production, sectoral relative prices play a more prominent role in the international transmission of shocks than traditional open-economy channels—such as exchange rate adjustments and substitution between domestic and for-eign goods. Our findings carry significant policy implications, particularly in the context of the cur-rent global trade conflicts. Specifically, shocks affecting the supply of foreign production factors can be highly inflationary and may lead to employment declines, even in relatively closed economies like the United States, due to their large market size. Moreover, U.S. de-mand shocks can disproportionately influence global price levels in an environment where factors of production in short supply are in high global demand. 43 References

## E.2 Aggregate Real Wages 

This subsection examines how closely the model is able to match real wage movements using our baseline quantification exercise, both in the aggregate and across sectors. We focus on the US and euro area since detailed wage data at the sector level is more readily available. We first examine the behavior of aggregate real wages. For the US, we use the non-farm business sector hourly compensation to measure nominal wages. These data come from FRED (code COMPNFB ). For the euro area, we obtained data on nominal wages in EuroStat (code D11 , sectors B-S). We deflate the series using headline CPI to obtain our measure of 58 real wages from the data. We similarly compute the real wage from the model by deflating the aggregate nominal wage by the overall price index. Figure E.2 compares the behavior of real wages in the data and those generated by baseline model quantification exercise by plotting the year-on-year growth rate of real wages. The black line represents the data, and the blue diamonds depict model predictions. The model-generated series tracks the evolution of real wages observed in the data quite closely over the analyzed period for the United States and euro area, and it is consistent with the large increase in the real wage during 2020 and its subsequent decline over 2021–2022.
